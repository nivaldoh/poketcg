# Building a Strong Pokémon TCG Agent: Top 2 Architectures for the Kaggle PTCG AI Battle Challenge

## TL;DR
- **Recommendation 1 (primary): a single small Transformer policy+value network trained by self-play with a regularization-toward-equilibrium loss (PPO/MMD-style or R-NaD/NeuRD), played at inference as one fast forward pass with NO tree search.** This directly fixes your miscalibrated value head and Lucario-only determinization, is provably less exploitable in imperfect-information games, and is the fastest possible thing at inference — which matters because the competition gives each player only a 10-minute cumulative clock per match (running out is an immediate loss).
- **Recommendation 2 (contrasting): keep search, but make it sound and cheap — an AlphaZero-style policy+value net used with a *redeterminizing* Information-Set MCTS (ISMCTS) that resamples hidden cards every iteration and uses a low, Gumbel-style simulation budget.** This is a modest, well-scoped upgrade to your existing MCTS that removes strategy-fusion/leakage bugs and the "always pick maxCount" defect while staying single-GPU and fast.
- Both keep your tiny Transformer and EmbeddingBag encoding. The decisive contrast is **search at training time only (Rec 1) vs. a little sound search at inference time (Rec 2)**; start with Rec 1 because it is simpler, faster, and most robust, and graft on Rec 2's ISMCTS only on high-leverage turns if your latency budget allows.

## Key Findings
- **The core challenge is imperfect information, and the textbook fix you used (single-determinization Perfect-Information Monte Carlo, PIMC) is known to be unsound.** It suffers from *strategy fusion* (the solver makes different choices in states the player cannot actually distinguish) and *non-locality* (sub-game values depend on the whole strategy, so unlikely worlds pollute the estimate). Your "mirror assumption" determinization compounds this against non-Lucario decks.
- **You do not need full poker-style equilibrium machinery to be competitive.** A 2025 large-scale study (Rudolph, Lichtlé, Sokota et al., "Reevaluating Policy Gradient Methods for Imperfect-Information Games," arXiv:2502.08938) reports: "Over 7000 training runs, we find that FP-, DO-, and CFR-based approaches fail to outperform generic policy gradient methods" — i.e., well-tuned PPO/PPG and Magnetic Mirror Descent reach *lower exploitability* and need no search at inference. DeepNash (Perolat et al., "Mastering the Game of Stratego with Model-Free Multiagent Reinforcement Learning," *Science* 2022; arXiv:2206.15378) mastered Stratego — a ~10^535-node imperfect-information game — with a model-free, search-free network at play time using Regularized Nash Dynamics (R-NaD): per Google DeepMind's blog its win rate against the best Stratego bots "topped 97%, and was frequently 100%," and it reached an all-time top-3 rank with an 84% win rate against human experts on Gravon.
- **This recipe already works for real collectible card games.** ByteRL/Hearthstone (Xiao, Zhang et al., "Mastering Strategy Card Game (Hearthstone) with Improved Techniques," arXiv:2303.05197, 2023) used end-to-end policy learning with optimistic smooth fictitious play and beat a Chinese-server top-10 Hearthstone streamer: "Our models defeat the human player in all Best-of-5 tournaments of full games (including both deck building and battle)."
- **Heavy "sound search" systems (ReBeL, Player of Games, Student of Games) are the theoretically right answer but are too heavy for a "keep it simple" single-GPU project.** They rely on public belief states / growing-tree CFR and large self-play infrastructure. Borrow their *idea* (train value/policy over beliefs; do search at train time) rather than implementing them.
- **The competition's time limit is a 10-minute cumulative clock per player per match, not a fixed per-move budget** (AICU coverage of the official rules: "each player's time limit is set to a maximum of 10 minutes. Since running out of time results in an immediate loss... overwhelming high-speed processing and real-time adaptability [is required]"). A pure forward-pass policy (Rec 1) uses almost no clock, and even modest ISMCTS (Rec 2) is affordable if you reserve search for pivotal decisions. Because a timeout is an instant loss, **worst-case latency, not average, is what you must bound.**
- **MuZero-family methods (MuZero, Stochastic MuZero, EfficientZero, Sampled/Gumbel MuZero) are tempting but mostly overkill here.** You already have a perfect simulator, so you do not need a *learned* dynamics model; the genuinely useful pieces are (a) Stochastic MuZero's chance-node handling for coin flips/draws and (b) Gumbel/Sampled MuZero's low-simulation, large-action-space search tricks — both can be borrowed without adopting full MuZero.

## Details

### 1. What this problem actually requires

The PTCG AI Battle Challenge (launched June 16, 2026 by The Pokémon Company with HEROZ and the University of Tokyo's Matsuo Institute — confirmed by @Pokemon_cojp, which describes the "cabt Engine" as "a proprietary Pokémon card battle simulator... developed by the University of Tokyo's Matsuo Institute specifically for the Kaggle environment") runs on `kaggle-environments`. Each decision point the agent receives an `Observation` with three parts — `logs` (event history), `current` (board state: your `active`, up to five `bench`, full `hand`, `discard`, `deckCount`, plus the opponent's *counts only* and face-down `prize` as `None`), and `select` (the legal `option` list) — and returns a list of integer indices into `select["option"]`, of length `maxCount`. Agents build a 60-card deck from a Standard-format pool of roughly 2,000 cards (TechRaptor, June 2026: The Pokémon Company will provide "a database of 2,000 cards from the game's Standard format"). Organizers publish daily episode-replay datasets, and state that rule-based programming alone is unlikely to score well.

Concretely, a strong agent must address:

1. **Imperfect information** — hidden opponent hand/deck contents and your own face-down prizes. This is *the* central difficulty and the thing PIMC handles unsoundly.
2. **Sequential multi-decision turns** — a turn is many chained micro-decisions (bench, attach, play trainers, evolve, attack, end). Credit assignment and action representation must work across long option sequences, and some decisions are multi-select (`maxCount > 1`).
3. **Large/variable/combinatorial action space** — the legal `option` set changes every step and can be large; you cannot enumerate full turns.
4. **Stochasticity** — shuffles, draws, and coin flips are genuine chance events.
5. **Generalization across decks** — you must not overfit to a single mirror; the meta has many archetypes.
6. **Value estimation** — your value head was trained on near-coin-flip mirror games, so it is miscalibrated; a useful value signal must come from diverse, non-mirror outcomes.
7. **Inference-time budget** — a 10-minute cumulative per-match clock; timeouts lose instantly, so bound worst-case per-move cost.

### 2. Why your current BC + determinized-MCTS approach is limited

Behavior cloning a rule-based Lucario teacher in mirror matches gives you (a) a policy that imitates one deck's tactics, (b) a value head calibrated only on ~50/50 mirror outcomes, and (c) a determinizer hard-wired to fill hidden cards from the Lucario list. Against other decks, the determinizer samples impossible worlds, and single-world PIMC then exhibits strategy fusion and non-locality. Your MCTS also always selects exactly `maxCount` options (wrong for variable selections), the policy head saw only single-select decisions, and the model/search are tiny/shallow. The scores are bounded scalars, not calibrated probabilities, so they cannot be mixed soundly with search statistics. These are exactly the failure modes the two recommendations target.

### 3. Recommendation 1 (primary): one small Transformer, regularized self-play, no inference-time search

**Core idea.** Keep your two-headed Transformer (sparse EmbeddingBag features → encoder value head in [-1,1]; decoder policy head scoring each legal `option`). Change the *training signal* from behavior cloning to **self-play reinforcement learning with explicit regularization toward an equilibrium**, and at play time just do **one forward pass and sample (or argmax) over the legal-action logits**. No tree search at inference.

**Why it fits.** In imperfect-information games, any deterministic, predictable policy is exploitable; you need a *stochastic* policy, and you want it to approximate a Nash equilibrium so it is robust to the whole field of opponent decks rather than tuned to one. Two practical, lightweight ways to get there:
- **Regularized policy gradient (simplest):** train with PPO/PPG-style updates in self-play, but add entropy/KL regularization toward a slowly-updated "anchor" policy. The 2025 study above found, over 7000+ runs, that well-tuned generic PG methods (PPO, PPG, Magnetic Mirror Descent) achieve *lower exploitability* than CFR-, fictitious-play-, and double-oracle-based deep RL. Magnetic Mirror Descent (Sokota et al., 2023) is essentially "policy gradient + KL-to-previous-policy" and converges toward quantal-response/Nash equilibria.
- **Regularized Nash Dynamics / NeuRD (the DeepNash recipe):** a "one-line change" to softmax policy gradient (skip the gradient through the softmax) plus reward transformation that regularizes toward the previous iterate. This is what powered DeepNash to expert human level in Stratego *with no search at inference*. Note a caveat for you: the same 2025 benchmark found R-NaD/NeuRD can *underperform* plain regularized PPO, attributed to NeuRD's less-mature update — so prefer regularized PPO/MMD first.

**How it handles imperfect information and search.** Hidden information is handled *implicitly*: the network conditions only on the legal observation (your hand/board + opponent counts + game log) and learns a mixed policy that is robust in expectation over hidden states — exactly DeepNash's stated advantage ("we do not need to explicitly model private states from public ones"). The equilibrium-seeking regularizer is what replaces search: the policy improvement that AlphaZero gets from a tree at runtime is instead baked into the weights during training. Stochasticity (coin flips/draws) is handled naturally because self-play samples those chance outcomes; the value head learns expected outcome over them.

**How it improves on / contrasts with your approach.** It deletes the broken determinizer entirely (no mirror assumption), fixes the miscalibrated value head by training on diverse self-play outcomes across many decks rather than coin-flip mirrors, trains the policy head on *all* decision types including multi-select, and yields a stochastic (less exploitable) policy. At inference it is the fastest option — a single forward pass — so it essentially never risks a timeout.

**Training notes / why it stays small and fast.** Reuse your `d_model=128`, 1–2 layer Transformer; you may widen modestly (e.g., d_model 192–256, 2–3 layers) since you have a single-GPU budget. Bootstrap from your existing BC weights (BC is a fine *initializer*), then run self-play with a pool of opponents: snapshots of your own agent plus a few scripted/archetype decks for diversity. Use action masking from the `select` list so illegal options get −∞ logits. Train the value head with a proper outcome-regression loss (win/lose/draw → [-1,1]) on full self-play games. Keep episodes cheap by exploiting the fast simulator. The main risks are self-play instability (mitigated by the KL/entropy regularizer and a slowly-moving anchor) and reward sparsity (mitigate with simple shaping like prize-card differential, used cautiously). This is squarely within a single-GPU, "test the core idea" scope.

### 4. Recommendation 2 (contrasting): sound, cheap inference-time search via redeterminizing ISMCTS with a Gumbel-style budget

**Core idea.** Keep search at inference, but replace single-world PIMC with **Information-Set MCTS (ISMCTS)**: build one tree over *information sets* (states indistinguishable to you), and **resample a fresh determinization of the hidden cards on every iteration** rather than committing to one world. Use your policy head as the PUCT prior and your value head (now properly trained) as the leaf evaluator. Cap the budget hard and allocate simulations with a **Gumbel/Sequential-Halving root rule** so a handful of simulations still yields a sound policy improvement.

**Why it fits.** ISMCTS was introduced precisely to cure PIMC's strategy fusion and budget-allocation problems by aggregating statistics in a single information-set tree, and it has been applied to imperfect-information card games including Pokémon (Ihara et al., 2018, found information sets beat plain determinization). Re-determinizing ISMCTS (RIS-MCTS) specifically fixes *information leakage* — the bug where one fixed determinization secretly reveals hidden cards to the search — and it won the 2018 Hanabi competition under strict time limits, exactly the "sound but fast" regime you need.

**How it handles imperfect information and search.** Each iteration samples a hidden-card assignment consistent with your information set (drawing the opponent's unknown cards from a *belief* over the legal card pool, not from your own deck), so no single impossible world dominates and the agent never "cheats" by conditioning on resampled hidden cards. Stochastic events (coin flips, draws) become explicit chance nodes; borrow Stochastic MuZero's **afterstate/chance-node** treatment — at a chance node, sample outcomes by their true probabilities rather than applying PUCT. For the large, variable action space, use **Sampled-MuZero-style action sampling** (expand only a sampled subset of legal options) and **Gumbel-Top-k + Sequential Halving** at the root, which empirically preserves policy improvement at very low simulation counts (e.g., ~5–25 sims) where vanilla MCTS degrades.

**How it improves on / contrasts with your approach.** Versus your current MCTS: (1) re-determinization replaces the wrong Lucario mirror assumption and removes leakage; (2) information-set aggregation removes strategy fusion; (3) the Gumbel root rule removes the "always exactly `maxCount`, low-count" pathology and makes few-simulation search principled; (4) the value head is now trained on diverse outcomes so leaf evaluations are meaningful (you can keep an occasional random rollout as a sanity backup). Contrast with Rec 1: this spends inference compute to *correct* the policy on the current board, which can catch tactical lines a pure forward pass misses — at the cost of latency and code complexity.

**Training notes / why it stays small and fast.** Same tiny Transformer; train it ideally with the **expert-iteration / AlphaZero loop** (search produces improved policy targets; network distills them; repeat) so that *search is amortized into the network*. This is the key efficiency lever: the better the prior/value net, the fewer simulations you need at runtime — you can even **distill the search policy back into the pure network and ship Rec 1 at inference**, doing search only during training. Bound worst-case latency by fixing a small simulation cap and reserving search for high-leverage turns (e.g., when top-2 policy logits are close or a KO/prize swing is at stake), acting in one forward pass otherwise. Belief modeling can start trivial (uniform over remaining legal cards given public counts and the ~2,000-card pool) and be refined later with simple opponent-deck priors learned from the daily replay exports.

### 5. Methods considered and deliberately *not* recommended (with the cheap idea to borrow)
- **ReBeL / Player of Games / Student of Games (public belief states, growing-tree CFR):** the theoretically sound RL+search frameworks for imperfect information, but they require belief-state networks and substantial self-play infrastructure — too heavy. *Borrow:* the principle that beliefs, not raw states, are what you search/learn over; and that value/policy should be trained against search-improved targets.
- **Full MuZero / EfficientZero (learned dynamics model):** unnecessary because the simulator is available and exact; learning a world model wastes capacity. *Borrow:* Stochastic MuZero's chance nodes (Rec 2) and Gumbel/Sampled MuZero's low-budget, large-action search (Rec 2).
- **Decision Transformer / return-conditioned BC and offline RL (CQL/IQL) on the replay exports:** a reasonable, simple offline baseline, and DT is just return-conditioned BC. But DT is still essentially BC and does not learn an equilibrium or stitch optimal play robustly; filtered/percentile BC (train only on winners' moves) is the cheapest worthwhile upgrade over your plain BC if you want a quick offline win before doing self-play. Keep these as baselines, not as the main bet.
- **LLM-minimax (PokéChamp):** powerful for Pokémon *video-game* battles by using an LLM for action proposal, opponent modeling, and value, with no training. But LLM calls are far too slow/heavy for a 10-minute-per-match clock over many decisions and a 2,000-card TCG. Not recommended here.

## Recommendations
1. **Stage 0 — fix the foundation (days):** Add action masking from `select`; retrain the value head as outcome regression; make the policy head handle multi-select decisions. Replace plain BC with **filtered BC** (imitate only winning trajectories from the daily replay exports across several decks, not just Lucario mirrors). This alone should beat your current agent and de-risk later stages.
2. **Stage 1 — build Recommendation 1 (primary):** Initialize from the filtered-BC weights and run **regularized self-play (PPO/PPG with entropy + KL-to-anchor; consider MMD)** against a diverse opponent pool (self snapshots + a few archetype decks). Ship the single-forward-pass stochastic policy. *This is your main submission.*
3. **Stage 2 — add Recommendation 2 selectively (optional, if latency allows):** Wrap the trained net in **redeterminizing ISMCTS with Gumbel-root, ~10–30 simulations**, chance nodes for coin flips/draws, and sampled-action expansion. Trigger search only on pivotal turns; act in one pass otherwise. Optionally run an **expert-iteration loop** and then **distill the search policy back into the network** so you can ship a pure-forward-pass agent that benefits from search done at training time.
- **Benchmarks that change the plan:** If self-play is unstable or win-rate against the archetype pool stalls below your filtered-BC baseline, increase the KL regularization / slow the anchor before adding capacity. If average per-move latency under ISMCTS threatens the 10-minute clock in long games, cut the simulation cap or restrict search to fewer trigger conditions, or fall back to the distilled pure-network policy. If exploitability/robustness across decks is the bottleneck, lean harder on Rec 1's equilibrium regularization rather than deeper search. Use head-to-head win rate against a held-out set of archetype decks (not mirror games) as your primary metric.

## Caveats
- **Source quality / recency:** Competition mechanics (10-minute per-match clock, ~2,000-card pool, observation format, daily replay exports) are drawn from the cabt Engine documentation plus secondary news coverage; some outlets disagree on minor figures (card pool quoted as "over 1,000" vs "2,000"; Simulation final-submission deadline Aug 16 vs Aug 17; total prize pool "$290,000+" vs "$300,000+"). Verify exact numbers against the official Kaggle rules before relying on them.
- **The "no-search PG beats CFR" result has limits:** the 2025 study's benchmark games were homogeneous (hidden-action, no chance, binary terminal rewards). Pokémon TCG has heavy chance and long turns, so equilibrium-convergence claims transfer only approximately. Treat Rec 1's robustness as strong-but-not-guaranteed and validate empirically.
- **R-NaD/NeuRD vs PPO:** despite DeepNash's fame, recent evidence suggests plain regularized PPO/MMD may be easier to tune and stronger than NeuRD at small scale; start there.
- **Self-play can converge to a narrow meta:** without a diverse opponent/deck pool it may overfit, repeating your original mirror problem in a subtler form. Maintain deck diversity and measure against held-out archetypes.
- **ISMCTS belief modeling is only as good as its prior:** a uniform belief over remaining cards is a safe start but a weak opponent model; improving it from replay data is worthwhile only if Stage 2 is paying off.
- **Latency is worst-case, not average:** because a single timeout loses the game, budget search for the longest plausible game, not the typical one.
