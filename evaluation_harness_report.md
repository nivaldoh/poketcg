# A Diagnostic Evaluation Harness for Game-Playing RL Agents: State of the Art in Evaluation and Debugging Methodology (with application to the PTCG AI Battle Challenge)

## TL;DR
- Build the harness as three decoupled layers — (1) algorithm-family-specific training telemetry, (2) policy-agnostic self-play dynamics evaluation, (3) policy-agnostic competence diagnosis of a fixed policy — because the field's most reliable insight is that a single win rate hides almost every failure mode you care about, and each layer localizes a different class of bug.
- The literature is mature and directly transferable: PPO correctness has canonical checklists (Huang et al., Engstrom et al., Andrychowicz et al.) and reference-game convergence tests (OpenSpiel exploitability on Kuhn/Leduc); self-play evaluation has Elo/Nash-averaging plus documented non-transitivity pathologies (Balduzzi et al., AlphaStar); and competence diagnosis has strong precedents (chess STS, KataGo adversarial blind spots, poker Local Best Response, value calibration via ECE). Pokémon-specific scaffolding already exists (Metamon, PokéChamp, the PokéAgent Challenge baselines).
- For a solo researcher: implement probe environments + a small solved-game convergence test first (days of effort, catches most PPO bugs), then a paired-seed league with bootstrapped Elo confidence intervals, then a hand-built PTCG challenge-position suite and a budgeted-rollout regret oracle. No published artifact combines league rating + challenge accuracy + phase regret + calibration into one "report card" for a game agent — that integration is a genuine gap you would be contributing.

## Key Findings

**1. Win rate is necessary but radically insufficient, and this is the field's central methodological consensus.** Andy Jones's "Debugging RL Without the Agonizing Pain" captures why: in an RL loop "errors aren't local" — a numerical bug in one place is "smeared throughout the system in seconds," so "most numerical errors manifest as all your metrics going weird at the same time," and "performance is noisy" so you "could write a bug-laden implementation and it might seem to work." The entire harness design should be understood as a machine for *localizing* failures that win rate merely signals.

**2. PPO's performance is dominated by implementation details, not the core algorithm.** Engstrom et al. (2020), "Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients," show code-level optimizations (value clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal init, learning-rate annealing, observation normalization/clipping) are "responsible for most of PPO's gain in cumulative reward over TRPO" and "fundamentally change how RL methods function." Huang et al. (2022), "The 37 Implementation Details of PPO" (ICLR Blog Track), enumerate them concretely, and Andrychowicz et al. (2020), "What Matters in On-Policy RL," implemented ">50 such 'choices' in a unified on-policy RL framework" and "train over 250'000 agents in five continuous control environments," finding PPO best in 4 of 5. Practical implication: a PTCG PPO baseline that underperforms is far more likely to have a details bug than an algorithm deficiency, so telemetry must be able to distinguish these.

**3. Self-play progress cannot be measured with a scalar in a non-transitive game, and PTCG is non-transitive** (type/archetype matchups are rock-paper-scissors-like). Balduzzi et al. establish the theory (gamescapes, relative population performance, Nash averaging) and AlphaStar operationalizes it (league with main/exploiter agents, Nash distribution over the league). Vinyals et al. (2019), "Grandmaster level in StarCraft II" (Nature), report that in the AlphaStar payoff matrix "there are around 3,000,000 rock–paper–scissor cycles (with requirement of at least 70% win rates to form a cycle) that involve at least one exploiter, and around 200 that involve only main agents" — the main agents behave largely transitively while exploiters are highly cyclic. Elo remains useful but must be reported with uncertainty and cross-checked against exploitability and cycle counts.

**4. Competence diagnosis has rich, replicable precedents you can port to PTCG:** curated challenge-position suites (chess STS: 1,500 themed positions, 15 categories), adversarial/blind-spot discovery (KataGo's cyclic vulnerability: a >97% adversary win rate at superhuman settings — and >99% with no tree search, trained with <14% of KataGo's compute — while the adversary is "easily beaten by human amateurs"), per-decision regret via a search oracle (chess ACPL; poker Local Best Response), and value-head calibration (reliability diagrams / Expected Calibration Error).

**5. There is no published integrated "diagnostic report card" for a game agent** combining rating + challenge accuracy + phase regret + calibration. The nearest precedents are HELM (multi-metric holistic evaluation), Model Cards (Mitchell et al. 2019), and CheckList (Ribeiro et al. 2020, per-capability behavioral testing). Combining them for a game agent is a contribution, not a lookup.

## Details

### Layer 1 — Implementation correctness and training telemetry (algorithm-family-specific)

#### 1a. Policy-gradient methods (plain PPO first) — the primary case

**Canonical references (read in full):**
- Engstrom et al. (2020), *Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients: A Case Study on PPO and TRPO*, arXiv:2005.12729; code at github.com/MadryLab/implementation-matters. Establishes that code-level optimizations dominate.
- Huang, Dossa, Raffin, Kanervisto, Wang (2022), *The 37 Implementation Details of Proximal Policy Optimization*, ICLR Blog Track (iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/); companion repo vwxyzjn/ppo-implementation-details. The single most actionable checklist; also documents invalid action masking (logits→−∞ before softmax) as used in AlphaStar/OpenAI Five and shown critical for Gym-µRTS — directly relevant since PTCG has large, state-dependent legal-action sets.
- Andrychowicz et al. (2020), *What Matters in On-Policy Reinforcement Learning?*, arXiv:2006.05990. Large-scale ablation; practical recommendations.
- CleanRL docs (docs.cleanrl.dev) and Stable-Baselines3 (the SB3 logger documents the exact telemetry fields below). CleanRL is the recommended single-file reference implementation to diff against.

**Reliable training-time health signals** (what each measures / what failure it localizes / cost / blind spot):
- **Policy entropy trajectory.** Measures exploration/collapse. Localizes premature convergence and, if it crashes to ~0 early, an exploration or reward-scale bug. Cost: trivial (already logged). Blind spot: healthy-looking entropy is compatible with a mis-specified reward.
- **Approximate KL per update** (Schulman's estimator; SB3 `approx_kl`). Measures step size between old/new policy. Localizes too-large steps (LR too high, advantage-scale bug) or dead updates (~0 KL = no learning). Cost: trivial. Blind spot: "Truly PPO" (arXiv:1903.07940) shows PPO cannot strictly bound the likelihood ratio — a reasonable proportion of ratios exceed the clip range (over 30% on Hopper/Reacher/Walker2d), so KL being "controlled on average" hides tail violations. Report max ratio, not just mean.
- **Clip fraction** (`clip_fraction`). Measures how often the ratio hits the clip threshold. Empirically starts high (~20%) and settles below ~10%; persistently near 0 means no effective constraint (LR too low / data stale), persistently high means steps too large. Cost: trivial. Blind spot: highly correlated with approx_kl, so it adds little beyond KL.
- **Value-function explained variance** (`explained_variance`; EV=1 perfect, EV=0 no better than predicting the mean, EV<0 worse than the mean). Measures critic quality; drives advantage accuracy. Localizes a broken critic, value/policy loss-scale imbalance, or wrong return targets. Cost: trivial. Blind spot: high EV on self-play data says nothing about calibration against *evaluation* opponents (see Layer 3).
- **Gradient norms.** Localize exploding/vanishing gradients, loss-scale imbalance, bad init. Cost: trivial. Blind spot: global norm can mask per-head problems — log policy-head and value-head norms separately.
- **Advantage statistics (mean/std pre- and post-normalization).** Localize the classic advantage-normalization bug. Cost: trivial. Blind spot: batch-level normalization interacts with minibatching.

**Documented bug "signatures"** (synthesized from Engstrom et al., the 37-details post, and Jones):
- *Wrong advantage normalization* (normalizing over the wrong axis / including bootstrap): unstable KL, clip fraction swinging, entropy collapse.
- *Value/policy loss-scale imbalance*: value loss dwarfs policy loss, gradient norm dominated by the value head, EV stagnant while policy entropy collapses.
- *Incorrect GAE or off-by-one in bootstrapping* (mishandling the terminal/`next_value` at episode boundaries): EV plateaus low; returns look biased near episode ends; often *the* bug in two-player/turn-based settings where "done" and reward attribution are subtle. Jones's central warning applies: because feedback is non-local, these present as *everything* going wrong at once.
- *Broken invalid-action masking*: entropy artificially high (probability mass on illegal actions), or the agent "cheats" — a PTCG-specific landmine given large legal-action variance across turns.

**Reference environments for unit-testing PG/self-play code (highest-leverage, cheapest tooling):**
- **Probe environments** (Jones; implemented in AgileRL's `agilerl.utils.probe_envs`). Tiny hand-built MDPs with known-correct value/policy outputs; "can be used to localise errors and confirm that algorithms are learning correctly." Run these first — they turn a non-local RL bug into a local unit-test failure.
- **Solved games with known Nash / exact exploitability via OpenSpiel (pyspiel):** `open_spiel.python.algorithms.exploitability.nash_conv` / `exploitability`. OpenSpiel's own tests assert e.g. Leduc-poker uniform-random NashConv = 4.7472…, Leduc always-fold NashConv = 2.0, Kuhn Nash equilibrium exploitability = 0. Kuhn poker, Leduc hold'em, and biased RPS / matrix games with known Nash are the standard convergence tests: point your self-play/PG loop at them and confirm exploitability→0 (or matches the known value). This is the single best correctness test for the self-play machinery independent of PTCG. Caveat from the OpenSpiel reproduction literature: neural (as opposed to tabular) estimators can "demonstrate inconsistent behavior and occasionally fail to improve," so use tabular convergence as the ground-truth gate.
- Recent methodological aids: *RLExplorer* (arXiv:2410.04322) toward automated fault detection in DRL programs; the NashPG paper (arXiv:2510.18183) documents the now-standard practice of computing exact exploitability with OpenSpiel on Kuhn/Leduc and approximating best response by training a fresh PPO exploiter for ~10,000 updates where exact BR is intractable — a template you can reuse for PTCG.

#### 1b. Behavior cloning — analogous correctness/telemetry story
BC reduces to supervised learning, so the "telemetry" is different: held-out action-prediction accuracy / top-k accuracy / cross-entropy on a validation split, and — critically — the same metrics *conditioned on state type / game phase* to expose coverage gaps. The characteristic failure is **compounding error under covariate shift**: Ross & Bagnell (2010), *Efficient Reductions for Imitation Learning*, show the learned policy's cost can grow quadratically with horizon T; DAgger (Ross, Gordon, Bagnell 2011) is the canonical fix (iteratively label states the learner actually visits). Telemetry that localizes it: measure prediction accuracy *on states the current policy visits* vs. *on expert/demonstration states* — a large gap is the BC-specific signature (off-distribution brittleness). For PTCG, the Metamon replay-reconstruction pipeline is the concrete BC/offline-RL data source; note its explicit "sim-to-real"-style gap between reconstructed (partially observed) trajectories and live play.

#### 1c. Search-based agents — analogous correctness/telemetry story
For MCTS/minimax agents (PTCG precedent: PokéChamp, a minimax agent powered by an LLM value/opponent model; and the "strong heuristic search engine" baselines), correctness telemetry is about the *search*, not gradients: node/second and effective branching factor; value-backup consistency (search value vs. realized outcome); sensitivity curves of playing strength vs. time/visits budget (should be monotone increasing — a non-monotone curve signals a search bug or value-function pathology); and simulator-fidelity checks (does the internal model reproduce the true simulator's transitions on random rollouts?). The KataGo adversarial work is the cautionary tale: search depth does not fix a mis-generalizing value function (the cyclic exploit persists even at superhuman search), so log value-vs-outcome calibration during search.

### Layer 2 — Evaluating self-play training dynamics (policy-agnostic)

**Progress metrics:**
- **Elo / TrueSkill / Glicko over a checkpoint league.** Standard, cheap, interpretable. Report *with* uncertainty: Glicko/Glicko-2 add a rating deviation ("to Elo what a confidence interval is to a point estimate," per Glickman) and volatility; TrueSkill handles multiway/teams via Bayesian inference. The AlphaStar Nature paper computed training Elo of every league player against the whole league.
- **Relative population performance & Nash averaging** (Balduzzi et al., *Re-evaluating Evaluation*, NeurIPS 2018, arXiv:1806.02643; *Open-ended Learning in Symmetric Zero-sum Games*, ICML 2019, arXiv:1901.08106). Nash averaging is *clone-invariant* — "repeated strategies in the game do not affect the ratings of the other strategies" — so it is not fooled by a league packed with near-duplicate checkpoints, which is exactly what naive self-play produces. A 2025 successor, *Deviation Ratings: A General, Clone-Invariant Rating Method* (arXiv:2502.11645), generalizes this and is worth tracking.
- **Exploitability / approximate exploitability** when exact best response is intractable (the PTCG case). Options in increasing cost: (i) exact via OpenSpiel on solved sub-games only; (ii) train a fresh RL "exploiter" against the frozen checkpoint (AlphaStar main/league exploiters; NashPG's PPO-exploiter recipe); (iii) *Approximate Exploitability: Learning a Best Response* (ISMCTS-BR, IJCAI 2022) which learns a best response and, near zero exploitability, notes "small absolute variations cause large" relative error — a real measurement blind spot.

**Known pathologies and how to detect them:**
- **Strategy cycling / non-transitivity.** Detect by building the full checkpoint payoff matrix and counting rock-paper-scissor cycles (as AlphaStar did — see the ~3,000,000-cycle figure above) and via Nash-cluster size. *Measuring the Non-Transitivity in Chess* (arXiv:2110.11737; MDPI Algorithms 2022) validates the "spinning top" geometry on 1B+ human games and concludes population-based training needs "large and diverse populations."
- **Population collapse / self-play overfitting to co-evolved opponents.** Detect via the AlphaStar validation-agent protocol: hold out agents trained to follow specific strategies and track the fraction that beat your main agent (they report the proportion of validation agents beating main agents in >80/160 games as a robustness signal). This is the direct antidote to "my agent only looks good against its own ancestors."
- **The Elo disagreement (present both sides).** One camp: Elo is a fine progress signal and correlates with strength (chess practice; AlphaStar training Elo). Opposing camp: Balduzzi et al. show Elo "cannot model intransitive interactions" — a one-dimensional skill number is provably inadequate in cyclic games, which is why they propose Nash averaging. Resolution for the harness: report Elo *and* a non-transitivity diagnostic (cycle count / Nash-cluster size); trust Elo only in the transitive regime.

**Low-variance evaluation protocols (essential given PTCG's high stochasticity — Metamon notes competitive Pokémon battles "can last more than one hundred stochastic turns"):**
- **Paired/mirrored seeds ("duplicate" evaluation)**, as in duplicate bridge/backgammon: play the same team matchups and RNG seeds with colors/positions swapped so both agents face identical luck; differences then reflect skill, not variance. Metamon standardizes team sets partly for this reason.
- **Variance reduction:** AIVAT-style / imaginary-observation control variates — in the LBR poker evaluation, duplicate hands with imaginary observations "reduce the size of the confidence intervals by roughly 20%"; at minimum, bootstrap confidence intervals over game logs.
- **How many games for a meaningful Elo gap:** rough power rule from the LLM-arena literature — detecting a 100-Elo gap (~64% win rate) at 80% power, α=0.05 needs ~150–200 games; Elo's original "Expressions of Reliability" table cites ~30 games→95%, ~100→99% confidence for coarser purposes. Best practice (LMArena; AlphaViT, arXiv:2408.13871): percentile bootstrap (e.g., B=1000 resamples) for 95% CIs on every rating, and treat overlapping CIs as statistically tied. Also adopt **rliable** (Agarwal et al., NeurIPS 2021, *Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice*, arXiv:2108.13264): report interquartile mean and stratified bootstrap intervals rather than point means across seeds.

### Layer 3 — Diagnosing a trained policy's competence (policy-agnostic)

**Challenge-position / puzzle suites (hand-built or mined "known-best-action" positions):**
- **Chess STS (Strategic Test Suite):** created 2008 by Dann Corbit and Swaminathan Natarajan — "a collection of 1500 positions, split into 15 themes of 100 positions each" (Undermining, Open Files and Diagonals, Knight Outposts, Square Vacancy, Bishop vs. Knight, center control, etc.), each with best moves scored 1–10 for a maximum of 15,000. Giraffe (arXiv:1509.01549) used it to track *positional understanding* during training (rose from ~6000 to ~9500/15000), and *Diversifying AI: Creative Chess with AlphaZero* (arXiv:2308.09175) uses STS (1088-position v6) plus Penrose/Lichess puzzle sets. The tooling (fsmosca/STS-Rating) shows the pattern: EPD positions + multipv engine analysis + interpolated scoring. **This is the direct template for a PTCG challenge suite** — categorize positions by skill (lethal/kill this turn, forced switch, setup vs. sweeper, prize-trade math, resource management) and score against a known-best action.
- **Go life-and-death / adversarial blind spots:** Wang et al. (2023), *Adversarial Policies Beat Superhuman Go AIs* (ICML; arXiv:2211.00241; goattack.far.ai), trained adversaries with a >97% win rate vs. KataGo at superhuman settings (and >99% against a no-search victim), using less than 14% of KataGo's compute, via a cyclic exploit "simple enough to be replicated by an amateur human" that transfers zero-shot to other Go AIs and persists after adversarial training — yet the adversaries are "easily beaten by human amateurs" because they "win by tricking KataGo into making serious blunders." Method: AlphaZero-style adversary with gray-box access to the victim network. The lesson for PTCG: actively *mine* blind spots by training an adversary against your frozen policy, not just curate hand positions — self-play champions have exploitable holes off their training distribution.
- **Poker / Hearthstone / MTG / Pokémon precedents:** poker uses exploitability and LBR (below); Hearthstone has the IEEE-CIG/AAIA "AI in Hearthstone" competitions and datasets; MTG has deck-/rules-AI research; **Pokémon has the most directly usable scaffolding** — Metamon (RLC 2025; arXiv:2504.04395; metamon.tech) provides "Datasets of 5M+ trajectories 'reconstructed' from real human battles and 20M+ generated by self-play," "40+ baseline policies ranging from beginner to expert-level human play," standardized team/opponent sets, and reports skill via win rate against a graded opponent suite plus ranked-ladder Glicko/GXE (its best agents reach the 90–99th percentile depending on ruleset). PokéLLMon (arXiv:2402.01118) and PokéChamp (minimax+LLM) are further baselines; and the NeurIPS 2025 PokéAgent Challenge (arXiv:2603.15563) releases "checkpoints from 30 agents that span the competitive skill ladder, ranging from compact RNNs to 200M-parameter Transformers," a dozen graded heuristic opponents, LLM/search baselines, and 3.5M+ full battle trajectories via metamon-parsed-replays — an off-the-shelf opponent ladder for Layer 2/3. (Note the Kaggle "PTCG AI Battle Challenge" is the Trading Card Game, distinct from Showdown battling, so port the *methodology* from Metamon/PokéAgent rather than the code directly.)

**Per-decision regret via a search oracle or budgeted rollouts (the harness's sharpest competence probe):**
- **Chess ACPL (average centipawn loss):** the archetypal regret-style metric — mean drop in engine evaluation per move. Precisely because you have a perfect PTCG simulator, you can compute an analogous **action-value gap**: for each decision, estimate Q(s, a_chosen) and max_a Q(s, a) by budgeted rollouts (or a stronger search oracle) and report the gap, *bucketed by game phase* (early setup / mid / lethal). **Documented critiques you must inherit:** (i) ACPL is regime-dependent — "losing c centipawns in a winning position is very different from losing c centipawns in an equal position" (arXiv:2302.13937), so a win-probability-based loss (as Lichess uses) is better than raw value loss; (ii) a single average hides distribution — "an ACPL of 50 could mean inaccuracies on almost every move or one big blunder" — so report the *distribution* and blunder counts, not just the mean; (iii) it depends on oracle depth/strength, so fix and report the rollout budget.
- **Poker Local Best Response (LBR)** — Lisý & Bowling (2017), *Equilibrium Approximation Quality of Current No-Limit Poker Bots*, arXiv:1612.07547 — is the key precedent for a *cheap lower bound on exploitability* that requires no exact best response. LBR "does not compute a best response strategy explicitly, but uses its local approximation to directly play against the evaluated strategy," maintaining a Bayes-updated belief over the opponent's range and looking only a couple of actions ahead with a heuristic value function. Its punchline is a direct warning about trusting head-to-head results: bots whose 2016 tournament gaps were tiny fractions of a blind per hand were shown to have "exploitability substantially larger than folding all hands" (always-fold is exploitable by ~750 mbb/hand), and "the bots that use card abstraction are losing over 3 big blinds per hand on average against their worst case opponent" (LBR lower bounds of ~2,600–4,700 mbb/g for Hyperborean, Slumbot, and Act1). The transferable design: a budgeted local-best-response probe against your PTCG policy gives a cheap *lower bound* on how exploitable it is, catching the "looks strong in the league, collapses to a targeted counter-strategy" failure that pure Elo misses.

**Value-function calibration:**
- Apply **reliability diagrams** and **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** to the value head: bin predicted win-probabilities, plot empirical win rate per bin (identity = perfect), and summarize as ECE = Σ (|B_m|/n)·|acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|. Points below the diagonal = overconfidence.
- **What miscalibration patterns indicate:** systematic overconfidence in specific state types → state-coverage gaps (positions under-represented in self-play); calibration that is good vs. self-play opponents but poor vs. the evaluation ladder → distribution shift between co-evolved and real opponents (the Layer-2 overfitting pathology, seen from the value side); value "lag" (confident far too late that a game is lost) is exactly the KataGo signature — its value head "remains confident of victory even many moves after it has irreversibly lost."
- **Known blind spots of ECE itself:** hard binning is sensitive to bin count and boundaries (SmoothECE and adaptive binning are proposed fixes); ECE can be low while the model is useless (a constant predictor at base rate); it is a marginal, not conditional, measure. Report reliability diagrams alongside the scalar.

**Behavioral / interpretability probes (lighter coverage, only replicable methods):**
- **Action-distribution entropy conditioned on state type** (e.g., entropy on "obvious lethal" turns should be low; high entropy there signals a competence hole). Cheap, replicable, and complements the challenge suite.
- **CheckList-style behavioral tests** (Ribeiro et al., ACL 2020, *Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP Models with CheckList*): minimum-functionality tests (does the agent always take a free knockout?), invariance tests (does an irrelevant board relabeling change the action?), and directional tests (does adding a threat shift the action the expected way?). This is the most transferable interpretability-adjacent methodology; heavier attribution/probing methods are not yet replicable enough to recommend for a solo build.

### Cross-cutting: benchmark design principles and the "report card" question

**Translating ML-evaluation principles to game agents:**
- **Construct validity** — does the metric measure the intended competence? A challenge suite must be validated so that scoring well genuinely requires the skill the category names (STS's design ethos; CheckList's capability matrix).
- **Discriminative power** — the suite must separate agents; use rliable interval estimates and Nash averaging so redundant opponents/positions don't inflate apparent progress.
- **Saturation** — once agents ace a category it stops informing; STS practitioners rotate/replace positions "no longer useful," a policy the harness should adopt.
- **Contamination** — challenge positions must never appear in training/self-play data (Giraffe and the AlphaZero-creativity work explicitly hold puzzles out); with mined positions, track provenance to avoid leakage.
- Foundational statistical hygiene: Agarwal et al. (rliable) — report IQM + stratified bootstrap CIs, not point means, given few seeds.

**Is there a precedent for a combined "diagnostic report card" for a game agent?** No single artifact combines {league rating + challenge-suite accuracy by category + regret by game phase + calibration}. The closest precedents to borrow *structure* from are: **HELM** (Liang et al. 2022, Stanford CRFM; multi-metric holistic evaluation — accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, efficiency measured together, principle of "multi-metric measurements"); **Model Cards for Model Reporting** (Mitchell et al. 2019, arXiv:1810.03993; a standardized nine-section report with disaggregated metrics + caveats & recommendations); **CheckList** (per-capability behavioral scorecard); and **Datasheets/Data Cards** for data provenance. A very recent preprint, *Rollout Cards* (arXiv:2605.12131, treat as unreviewed), proposes a per-rollout reproducibility artifact for agent research. **Conclusion: building the integrated game-agent report card is a genuine, publishable gap** — you would be composing established components (Elo+Nash averaging, STS-style category accuracy, LBR/ACPL-style phase regret, ECE calibration) into a Model-Card-structured artifact.

**Using a shared suite to choose between algorithm families** (self-play RL vs. BC vs. search): there is no single canonical paper doing exactly this as a *decision procedure*, but Metamon provides the closest worked example — it evaluates IL, offline RL, offline-fine-tuned-on-self-play, an LLM agent, and a heuristic search engine on one shared opponent/team benchmark, finding the LLM agent "uncompetitive in the long horizons of the early generations" and the offline-RL sequence model matching/surpassing the strong search engine. The measurements that best expose each family's characteristic failure:
- **BC's off-distribution brittleness** → the visited-state vs. demonstration-state accuracy gap (Layer 1b) and challenge-suite accuracy on rare/edge positions (Layer 3); BC will score well on common positions and fall off a cliff on mined rare lines.
- **Model-free RL's sample inefficiency on rare lines** → per-category challenge accuracy and phase regret on low-frequency situations; RL will show high variance and slow improvement there even as overall Elo climbs.
- **Search's dependence on simulator fidelity and time budget** → the strength-vs-budget monotonicity curve and simulator-fidelity checks (Layer 1c); search collapses when the internal model diverges from the true simulator or under tight time limits, invisible to a fixed-budget win rate.
Early, cheap experiments (a small challenge suite + a budgeted-regret probe + a strength-vs-budget sweep) can therefore forecast which family is worth scaling before committing compute.

## Recommendations (prioritized build plan for a solo researcher with modest compute)

**Stage 0 — Correctness gates (days; do before any training run you trust).**
1. Implement Jones-style **probe environments** (or reuse AgileRL's) and make them CI unit tests. *Read in full:* Jones, "Debugging RL Without the Agonizing Pain."
2. Point your self-play/PPO loop at **Kuhn poker, Leduc hold'em, and a biased matrix game via OpenSpiel**, and assert exploitability→known values using `nash_conv`/`exploitability`. This is the highest-leverage correctness test. *Skim:* OpenSpiel exploitability code/tests; NashPG for the exploiter recipe.
3. Wire standard **PPO telemetry** (entropy, approx_kl + max ratio, clip fraction, EV, per-head grad norms, advantage stats) with the bug-signature interpretations above. *Read in full:* Huang et al. "37 Details"; *skim:* Engstrom et al., Andrychowicz et al.
Benchmark to advance: probe envs pass and a solved game reaches near-zero exploitability. If not, stop — you have a Layer-1 bug.

**Stage 1 — Low-variance league evaluation (1–2 weeks).**
4. Build a **checkpoint league** with **paired/mirrored-seed (duplicate) evaluation** and **bootstrapped 95% CIs on Elo** (percentile bootstrap, B≈1000); treat overlapping CIs as ties. Budget ~150–200 games per pair for a ~100-Elo resolution. Adopt **rliable** IQM/interval reporting across seeds.
5. Add **non-transitivity diagnostics**: full payoff matrix, RPS-cycle count, Nash-cluster size, and **Nash averaging** for a clone-invariant rating. *Read in full:* Balduzzi et al. *Re-evaluating Evaluation*; *skim:* AlphaStar Nature paper (league + validation-agent robustness protocol), Deviation Ratings (2025).
6. Add the **held-out validation-agent** protocol (scripted/archetype opponents never used in self-play) to detect co-evolution overfitting.
Benchmark to advance: Elo rising *and* validation-agent win rate rising *and* cycle count not exploding. Divergence between these is your early-warning signal.

**Stage 2 — Competence diagnosis of a fixed policy (2–4 weeks, iterative).**
7. Hand-build a **PTCG challenge-position suite** (STS pattern): categories like forced lethal, prize-trade math, setup vs. disruption, resource/energy management, mulligan/opening; each with a known-best action and partial-credit scoring; hold out from training; rotate saturated positions. *Read in full:* STS wiki + fsmosca/STS-Rating; *skim:* Creative-Chess-with-AlphaZero.
8. Implement a **budgeted-rollout regret oracle** using the perfect simulator: per-decision action-value gap in **win-probability units**, bucketed by game phase, reported as a distribution + blunder count (not just a mean). Inherit the ACPL critiques.
9. Add an **LBR-style local-best-response probe** as a cheap exploitability lower bound against frozen checkpoints; optionally a KataGo-style **trained adversary** to mine blind spots. *Read in full:* Lisý & Bowling (LBR); *skim:* Wang et al. adversarial-Go.
10. Add **value-head calibration**: reliability diagrams + ECE, computed separately vs. self-play and vs. the evaluation ladder to expose distribution shift; log value-vs-outcome over the game to catch KataGo-style late overconfidence.
11. Add cheap **behavioral probes**: state-conditioned action entropy; a handful of CheckList-style invariance/directional tests.

**Stage 3 — Integrate into a report card and use it for the family decision (ongoing).**
12. Compose Stages 1–2 outputs into a **Model-Card-structured "diagnostic report card"** (rating + CIs, challenge accuracy by category, phase-bucketed regret distribution, calibration diagrams/ECE, non-transitivity summary, and per-family failure indicators). This is the novel artifact.
13. Run the **cheap family-comparison battery** (small challenge suite + budgeted-regret probe + search strength-vs-budget sweep + BC visited-vs-demonstration gap) on a PPO baseline, a BC baseline (Metamon-style), and a search baseline (PokéChamp-style) to forecast which family to scale.

**Thresholds that should change the plan:** if solved-game exploitability won't converge → Layer-1 bug, do not proceed. If Elo rises but validation-agent win rate stalls or cycle count explodes → self-play overfitting/cycling; switch to prioritized/league opponent sampling. If challenge accuracy is high but ladder win rate is low → construct-validity or contamination problem in the suite. If value calibration is good on self-play but poor on the ladder → distribution shift; broaden self-play opponent diversity. If an LBR/adversary probe finds a cheap exploit → the champion has a blind spot regardless of Elo; add the exploit line to the challenge suite and to opponent sampling.

## Caveats
- **Source-quality flags:** the PPO-telemetry "typical value" ranges (clip fraction ~20%→<10%, EV stabilizing ~0.75) come from practitioner blog posts/SB3 docs, not controlled studies — treat as heuristics, not thresholds. Elo "games needed for confidence" figures blend a rigorous LLM-arena power calculation with Elo's original coarse reliability table; use the bootstrap-CI approach as the rigorous version. Several cited items are very recent preprints that may not be peer-reviewed (Deviation Ratings 2025; PokéAgent Challenge and Rollout Cards, 2026-dated arXiv IDs; other 2026-dated IDs surfaced in search) — flagged in-text; verify before relying on them.
- **The Kaggle "PTCG AI Battle Challenge" is the Trading Card Game, distinct from Pokémon *battling* (Showdown/VGC).** The Metamon/PokéAgent ecosystem is the richest transferable methodology and opponent-ladder precedent, but its code targets Showdown; port the evaluation *design*, not the environment. I did not locate public writeups specific to the Kaggle PTCG competition's internals; if they exist, prioritize the official competition report for its exact simulator API and scoring.
- **Non-transitivity means no metric is complete alone.** The Elo-usefulness disagreement is genuine and unresolved in the literature; the harness deliberately reports Elo *and* Nash averaging *and* cycle counts *and* exploitability probes precisely because each is blind to a different pathology. Do not collapse the report card back into a single number.
- **Regret and LBR oracles are only as good as their budget/heuristics.** Budgeted rollouts under-estimate the true best action in deep tactical lines; LBR gives a *lower* bound on exploitability (real exploitability is at least as bad). Fix and report all budgets.
